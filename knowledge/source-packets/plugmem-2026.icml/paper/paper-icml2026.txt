A. Motivation, Research Questions, and Novelty
A.1. Background and Motivation
Recent advances in LLM-based agents have highlighted the critical role of external memory systems in enabling long-horizon
reasoning and decision-making. However, existing memory paradigms in both academic research and engineering practice
exhibit a fundamental limitation: they adopt fragmented and incompatible definitions of memory (Li et al., 2025; Kang
et al., 2025; Chhikara et al., 2025).
Specifically, prior work typically focuses on only one of the following two forms of memory:

   • Factual (semantic) memory: summaries of past experiences in the form of facts, such as user preferences, historical
     events, or world knowledge (Huang et al., 2026; Gutiérrez et al., 2025; Sarthi et al., 2024).

   • Procedural memory: distilled knowledge of how to perform tasks, often represented as workflows, action sequences,
     or strategies derived from past executions (Wang et al., 2024b; Fang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024).

These two types of memory are often treated in isolation. For example, conversational agents emphasize factual memory
about users, while web agents and embodied agents prioritize procedural knowledge such as action workflows. As a result,
existing systems are typically designed around a single memory type, leading to solutions that are inherently task-specific
and difficult to generalize.
However, in real-world agent scenarios, both types of memory are indispensable and inherently complementary (Tulving,
1972; Squire, 2004). Consider a personal assistant tasked with purchasing a branded wallet on behalf of a user. To successfully
complete this task, the agent must rely on procedural knowledge, such as how to navigate different shopping websites,
understand their layouts, search for products, apply filters, and complete the checkout process; each of which may vary
significantly across platforms. At the same time, the agent must also leverage factual knowledge about the user, including
preferences for color, style (e.g., minimalist versus ornate), and acceptable price range. These user-specific facts are crucial
for selecting a product that satisfies the user’s expectations. Therefore, effective decision-making requires not only access to
both types of memory, but also their dynamic interaction within a unified framework.
Despite their complementary nature, current memory systems lack a unified framework to jointly model, retrieve, and utilize
these heterogeneous memory types. This fragmentation leads to a critical limitation: existing methods cannot effectively
handle scenarios where multiple memory types must be jointly leveraged.

A.2. Research Question
Motivated by this gap, we ask the following central research question:

     Can we design a unified, task-agnostic memory module for LLM agents that simultaneously supports both factual
     and procedural memory, and enables their effective interaction for decision-making?

This question is practically important for two reasons:

   • Any non-trivial agent task requires both “knowing that” (facts) and “knowing how” (procedures).

   • These two forms of knowledge must not only coexist, but also interact dynamically during task execution.

Importantly, the challenge is not merely to apply existing systems to multiple benchmarks. A possible misunderstanding is
to attribute the limited applicability of prior methods to incomplete evaluation, i.e., that these systems could potentially
generalize if only they were tested on more diverse benchmarks. However, this is not the case. Rather, prior methods are
practically constrained by their design: they are typically built around a single type of memory, with representations,
abstractions, and retrieval mechanisms tailored specifically to that setting (Wang et al., 2024b; Gutiérrez et al., 2025).
As a result, these systems are not simply under-evaluated on other memory types, but they are structurally incompatible
with them. Even with substantial engineering effort to adapt such systems to a different class of memory tasks, their

performance remains inherently limited. This is because factual and procedural memory differ not only in surface form, but
also in their underlying structure, usage patterns, and role in decision-making.
For example, consider a memory module designed for long-horizon conversational benchmarks, where the primary goal is to
summarize a user’s past interactions and extract persistent factual information such as preferences or personal history. Such
systems are optimized for aggregating and updating user-specific facts from dialogue. In contrast, web agent tasks require a
fundamentally different type of memory: the agent must extract and reuse successful action workflows from past trajectories,
enabling it to perform better in future interactions by leveraging procedural knowledge. The capabilities evaluated in these
two settings are inherently different, as they emphasize distinct forms of memory abstraction and usage. As a result, a
memory system designed for one type of benchmark cannot be effectively transferred to the other, even with significant
modification. In other words, methods optimized for one type cannot be easily repurposed to the other without breaking
their core design assumptions.
Therefore, the limitation of prior work is not a matter of evaluation scope, but a consequence of task-dependent memory
definitions and architectures, which prevent them from effectively supporting heterogeneous memory types within a
unified framework.

A.3. Cognitive Science Inspiration
To address this challenge, we draw inspiration from cognitive science, which provides a principled and widely validated
framework for understanding human memory and knowledge (Anokhin et al., 2024).
A long-standing distinction in cognitive science characterizes knowledge into two fundamental types (Mokyr, 2012):

   • “Knowing that”: declarative, factual knowledge (semantic memory)

   • “Knowing how”: procedural, action-oriented knowledge (procedural memory)

This distinction has been extensively studied in philosophy, psychology, and related fields, and has been used to explain
human learning, reasoning, and decision-making processes.
We further note that this perspective is not merely theoretical, but has also been reflected in modern analyses of technological
and economic progress. For example, the 2025 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences (Nobel Prize Outreach, 2026), awarded to
Joel Mokyr, Philippe Aghion, and Peter Howitt, highlights how long-term growth is driven by innovation and the process of
creative destruction, where new technologies and methods replace outdated ones. In their frameworks, sustained progress
relies not only on discovering that something works (factual understanding), but also on developing and refining how to apply
it effectively (procedural capability). The Nobel committee explicitly emphasizes that successful innovation requires both
the accumulation of knowledge about the world and the ability to operationalize that knowledge into improved production
processes and actions.
This provides a concrete, real-world example supporting the generality of the “knowing that” and “knowing how” distinction,
and motivates its application to the design of agent memory systems.
We adopt this perspective as a guiding principle: agent memory should be organized around knowledge types that
directly support decision-making, rather than raw experience alone.

A.4. Our Approach: Unified Memory via Knowledge Abstraction
Based on this principle, we propose a unified approach that treats memory as knowledge extracted from experience.
Specifically, we:

   • Transform heterogeneous raw experiences into structured knowledge representations via memory-to-knowledge
     abstraction.

   • Represent both factual and procedural knowledge within a unified knowledge-centric memory graph.

   • Enable joint retrieval and reasoning over these knowledge types, allowing them to interact dynamically during
     decision-making.

A key design requirement is that these knowledge types must coexist without conflict and be jointly usable. For example,

A.5. Interpretation of the Title
Our title reflects the core properties of the proposed system:

Task-Agnostic Existing memory systems are often designed for specific benchmarks or task settings, resulting in
fragmented definitions of memory. In contrast, we define memory in a task-independent manner: any past experience
that can be abstracted into useful knowledge to guide future decision-making should be supported. Our goal is to build a
memory module that generalizes across diverse agentic tasks without redesign.

Plugin Practical usability is a central design goal. A memory module should be easily deployable across different agent
systems without requiring task-specific redesign or intrusive modifications to the agent architecture. In this work, we
emphasize an extreme level of engineering simplicity and generality: regardless of the agent’s task (including both factual-
and procedural-memory-intensive scenarios), our module can be integrated with minimal effort.
Concretely, our implementation supports a lightweight integration that requires only a few lines of code (e.g., six lines) to i)
import the module, ii) initialize memory, iii) perform memory updates, and iv) enable retrieval and memory reasoning during
agent execution. This means that developers can seamlessly augment an existing agent with a fully functional memory
system without redesigning task pipelines or adapting the system to specific benchmarks.
This level of plug-and-play usability represents a significant engineering contribution, ensuring that the proposed memory
module is not only theoretically general, but also practically deployable across a wide range of real-world agent systems.

Memory Module We emphasize that memory is not merely storage, but a functional and dynamic process that transforms
experience into decision-relevant knowledge, retrieves it when needed, and adapts it to the current task context.
Our design reflects the view that a memory module must go beyond simply storing or aggregating different types of memory.
Instead, it should provide a principled and unified processing pipeline that can handle heterogeneous memory types while
enabling their interaction during decision-making. In particular, we identify three essential components:

   • Structuring: A principled abstraction process that transforms raw experiences into a unified representation, organizing
     both factual (“knowing that”) and procedural (“knowing how”) memory within the same framework.
   • Retrieval: A task-driven mechanism that is agnostic to memory type, allowing relevant knowledge to be accessed
     based on the current task requirements rather than predefined memory categories.
   • Reasoning: A dynamic integration process that combines retrieved knowledge across different memory types, distilling
     them into a coherent, task-aligned representation to support decision-making.

Crucially, these components operate as a cohesive and interdependent pipeline, rather than a simple aggregation of separate
systems for different memory types. The structuring stage establishes a unified representation, retrieval selects task-relevant
knowledge without bias toward a specific type, and reasoning enables dynamic interaction and fusion of heterogeneous
knowledge. Together, they form a memory module that is inherently dynamic, compositional, and decision-oriented,
rather than a static repository or a loosely coupled combination of independent subsystems.

A.6. Discussion of Key Design Aspects and Novelty
The novelty of our work lies in rethinking agent memory from a fundamentally unified and principled perspective, rather than
introducing isolated improvements to existing paradigms. Prior work typically treats memory as either factual summaries or
procedural traces, and builds systems tailored to one of these forms (Wang et al., 2024b; Gutiérrez et al., 2025). In contrast,
we identify that this separation is itself the core limitation, and propose a unified formulation in which both factual (“knowing
that”) and procedural (“knowing how”) knowledge are treated as first-class citizens within a single memory system. This
is not merely a matter of combining two types of memory, but of designing a representation and processing pipeline that
allows them to coexist, be retrieved under a common mechanism, and interact dynamically to support decision-making.

In Hop0, the retriever typically surfaces a small set of low-level propositions that are semantically close to the question

the corpus. In both examples, the first hop retrieves a bridge-bearing statement; the next hop uses the bridge to directly
access the answer-bearing statement (birth year or first-launch year), while irrelevant but superficially related candidates
(e.g., other payload specialists, other singer-songwriters) are naturally deprioritized once the bridge entity is fixed.

retrieval granularity. Flat retrieval systems can surface past trajectories or similar task descriptions, but these memories are
typically either too specific (tied to particular pages or layouts) or too verbose to be directly actionable. As a result, the
agent must internally interpret and reconcile large amounts of loosely relevant information, leading to context explosion
and brittle decision making. In contrast, P LUG M EM retrieves procedural knowledge units—such as category navigation,
constraint-based filtering, and ranking under partial observability—and further compresses them through its reasoning
module into concise, decision-aligned instructions. The benefit is not simply fewer tokens, but a sharper alignment between
retrieved memory and the agent’s immediate control decision.
On the other end of the spectrum, task-specific web agents such as AWM and SteP hard-code or accumulate a single
canonical workflow per domain. While effective when tasks closely match the assumed structure, such workflows lack the
ability to adapt their internal logic as the task unfolds. This case highlights that web navigation tasks are not monolithic:
the agent alternates between qualitatively different reasoning regimes (e.g., locating the correct semantic scope, enforcing
numeric constraints, and resolving ties under incomplete information). A static workflow cannot anticipate these shifts.
Although AWM updates its workflow after task completion, it does not revise guidance within a task or condition it on
intermediate observations. P LUG M EM, by contrast, recomputes guidance at each step, allowing the agent’s strategy to
evolve as the environment reveals new structure.

B.2. The Role of Knowledge-Centric Structuring in Memory Effectiveness
Our structuring module serves as the core component that enables memory-to-knowledge abstraction, transforming raw
experience into explicit, decision-relevant knowledge units. To better understand its impact, we use case studies to examine
how this design differs from the most closely related line of prior work, i.e., summarization-style memory abstraction.
Existing methods in this category also perform a form of abstraction over past experience. However, instead of organizing
memory into explicit knowledge units, they typically produce higher-level summaries of interactions, such as notes (Xu
et al., 2025), compressed descriptions (Huang et al., 2026), or iteratively refined abstractions of the main content (Sarthi
et al., 2024; Chhikara et al., 2025). These representations may capture the overall topic or salient points of past experience,
but they do not explicitly align with the underlying structure of knowledge required for decision-making.

C. Related Work
C.1. Cognitive Science Based Agent Memory
Cognitive science has long studied the mechanisms of human memory, providing valuable insights into how memory can be
leveraged in agents. The Atkinson–Shiffrin memory model defines long-term memory as information that can be retained
and recalled over extended periods of time (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968). Long-term memory is further categorized into three
major types (Tulving, 1972; Squire, 2004): semantic memory, which stores concepts and factual knowledge; procedural
memory, which is responsible for knowing how to perform actions and skills; and episodic memory, which represents raw
records of personal experiences and events. For intelligent agents, most interactions with the environment are episodic
memory, such as raw contexts and working trajectories. From these episodic experiences, agents can extract more reusable
representations. For example, user preferences can be distilled as semantic memory (Li et al., 2025), while web navigation
skills can be abstracted as procedural memory (Wang et al., 2024b). In this sense, semantic and procedural memories are
not isolated components, but rather structured abstractions derived from episodic memory. Knowledge can be viewed as
a further abstraction over memory. Specifically, propositional knowledge corresponds to highly structured and verifiable
abstractions of semantic memory, while prescriptive knowledge represents generalized and reusable forms of procedural
memory that describe how to accomplish goal-oriented tasks (Mokyr, 2012).
Inspired by these cognitive theories, many studies have explored memory mechanisms for intelligent agents. Episodic
and semantic memory have been introduced to handle long-context information, such as long documents in document
understanding agents (Lee et al., 2024) and long-term user–agent interactions in conversational agents (Li et al., 2025).
Additionally, some approaches construct knowledge graphs containing both episodic and semantic subgraphs to better
organize and manage agent memory (Anokhin et al., 2024; Rasmussen et al., 2025). Procedural memory has also been
incorporated into decision-making agents, particularly web agents, where accumulated experiences are leveraged to improve

C.2. Memory Module Designs
Across agentic systems, external memory is most commonly realized through retrieval. Agents store past interactions,
observations, or documents outside the model context and retrieve relevant information at inference time to condition
decision making. This retrieval first paradigm forms the basic prototype of external memory, and nearly all subsequent
memory designs can be viewed as extensions built on top of it.
Early task agnostic memory systems adopt flat retrieval over unstructured episodic memory. Vanilla retrieval and vanilla
RAG store raw interaction histories or documents as text and retrieve relevant segments based on similarity to the current
query (Lewis et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024a). These methods provide a simple and broadly applicable memory interface,
but the retrieved content remains tightly bound to specific episodes. As a result, retrieved memories often contain redundant
details and exhibit low information density when reused across different decision contexts.
To improve retrieval effectiveness, later work introduces structure on top of episodic memory. Graph based and hierarchical
retrieval systems organize stored content to support multi hop retrieval, aggregation, or global reasoning (Edge et al., 2025;
Sarthi et al., 2024). Related approaches further refine retrieval by altering the internal organization of memory graphs, such
as ranking paths or linking propositions (Gutiérrez et al., 2025; Wang & Han, 2025). While these methods differ in structure
and traversal strategy, they largely preserve the same retrieval oriented abstraction. Memory remains organized around
episodic traces, entities, or text spans, and structural mechanisms primarily serve to improve access to stored experiences
rather than to transform their content.
In parallel, task-specific memory systems explore more aggressive forms of memory processing. Temporal knowledge
graphs for conversational agents, workflow memories for web navigation, and reasoning oriented memory banks explicitly
extract higher level representations from experience (Gutiérrez et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024b; Ouyang et al., 2025; Zhang
et al., 2025). These systems demonstrate that abstracting experience into reusable representations can substantially improve
performance within a fixed task setting. However, the abstraction process is typically guided by task-specific assumptions,

C.3. Memory Benchmarks and Evaluation Scope
A broad range of benchmarks have been used to study memory related behaviors in language models and agents, spanning
diverse task settings and evaluation objectives. One prominent line of work focuses on long context utilization, evaluating
whether models can access and reason over large inputs within a single inference window, such as long document
understanding, narrative comprehension, or needle in a haystack style retrieval (Liu et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2024; Zhang et al.,
2024). These benchmarks assess context usage and attention behavior, but do not model memory accumulation, update,
or reuse across time. A separate line of work focuses on long-horizon conversational agents and user-centric memory,
evaluating whether systems can accumulate, maintain, and reuse information such as preferences, personal attributes, and
dialogue context over extended interactions (Wu et al., 2024; Maharana et al., 2024). These benchmarks explicitly stress
episodic accumulation and robustness to redundancy and noise, and are commonly used to study memory abstraction in
interactive settings. Another class of benchmarks evaluates retrieval-augmented question answering over static knowledge
sources, including factoid and multi-hop QA tasks, where memory is treated as a fixed external repository and the primary
challenge lies in retrieval and evidence composition rather than memory evolution (Petroni et al., 2021; Kwiatkowski
et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2018; Trivedi et al., 2022). While effective for studying semantic retrieval and composition, these
benchmarks assume a static memory base and do not capture how memory is constructed or refined through interaction. In
agentic settings, additional benchmarks evaluate end-to-end task performance in interactive environments, including web
navigation, tool use, and embodied reasoning, where memory interacts with planning, perception, and action execution (Zhou
et al., 2024; Trivedi et al., 2024; Furuta et al., 2024; Shridhar et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2025). In such benchmarks, memory is
often entangled with other agent capabilities, making it difficult to isolate memory-specific contributions.

Semantic Given a standardized episodic unit et , we extract semantic memories as proposition–tag pairs. We query an LLM
using the prompt in Listing 5, which returns up to Nmax = 10 items in a fixed schema. Each item contains a Statement
field (a proposition in the main paper) and a Tags field (a list of associated concepts). We deterministically parse the LLM
output into (p, T ) pairs, where p is the proposition text and T is the corresponding concept set.
We materialize these outputs into the semantic graph GS by creating a low-level proposition node for each p and high-level
concept nodes for each c ∈ T , caching dense embeddings and metadata for all nodes. We add two edge types: i) membership
edges linking propositions to their concept tags (p → c), and ii) provenance edges linking each proposition back to its
source episodic unit in the episodic graph GE (p → et ). When multiple propositions originate from the same episodic unit,
we additionally connect them with sibling edges to preserve local co-occurrence structure.

D.3. Retrieval with Interleaved Abstraction and Specificity
This appendix provides implementation-oriented details that complement the high-level description in Section 3.2. P LUG -
M EM performs interleaved multi-hop retrieval over the semantic graph GS and procedural graph GP by alternating between
i) abstraction-level routing through concept/intent nodes and ii) specificity-level expansion over proposition/prescription
nodes. Given a query Q, the retriever is orchestrated by three LLM prompts: G ET M ODE (Listing 8) selects the memory
type(s) to emphasize; G ET P LAN (Table 9) proposes the next-hop abstract signals (concept tags for GS or subgoals/intents
for GP ); and M ULTI H OP C TRL (Listing 10) serves as a retrieval controller that decides whether the current evidence is
sufficient to stop, or which low-level nodes to prioritize in the next hop.
At hop t, retrieval starts from a low-level candidate pool Ct . We first expand candidates through two complementary
channels. (Embedding channel) We embed the current query text Qt and retrieve a set of high-scoring low-level nodes
by dense similarity. (Linking channel) In parallel, we run G ET P LAN to produce an abstract plan (concept tags for GS or
intents/subgoals for GP ), match it to the corresponding high-level nodes, and activate their adjacent low-level neighbors via
membership edges. The two candidate sets are merged (union), then reranked and pruned back to a fixed budget (top-K)
using a value function (primarily relevance, optionally combined with metadata such as importance/recency). Throughout
the process, high-level nodes are used only as intermediate routing signals and are not retained in Ct .
After obtaining the budgeted candidate set, we invoke M ULTI H OP C TRL to assess sufficiency. Concretely, M ULTI H OP C TRL
returns a strict JSON object with an enough flag and an optional list of selected low-level node IDs (top node ids),
constrained to be a subset of the available candidates; if enough=true, it returns an empty list. If enough=true,
retrieval terminates early and the current candidates are returned (or mapped to provenance-linked episodic nodes when

D.5. Full Operations on Memory Graph
Setup. We conduct multiple rounds of controlled experiments on the HotpotQA-induced semantic subgraph. We choose
HotpotQA for two practical reasons: i) its evidence is largely factual and extractive, which makes semantic memories
comparatively stable and less sensitive to stylistic variance; and ii) the resulting subgraph is lightweight enough to support
fast iteration over different merge thresholds and update policies.
Given a semantic node, our update routine first performs candidate discovery by collecting all semantic nodes that share
at least one concept/tag with it (i.e., neighbors under the tag-induced projection). We then compute embedding similarity
between the current node and each candidate, rank candidates by similarity, and select the top-m candidates whose
similarity exceeds a pre-defined threshold τ (we use m=1 by default, i.e., only the most similar candidate above threshold is
considered). If a pair (si , sj ) is selected, we trigger a merge operation by calling an LLM to i) synthesize a new semantic
memory that better summarizes the combined information, and ii) decide whether to deactivate the original nodes (delete
neither, delete both, etc.) according to explicit rules encoded in the prompt; the full prompt is shown in Listing 14.

E. Information-Theoretic Evaluation of Memory Efficiency
We present a framework to quantify the utility of generated memory. We decompose memory efficiency into two dimensions:
accuracy (Pointwise) and certainty (Distributional), and provide a unified interpretation of their connections.

  • Optimal Action (a∗ ): Let a∗ ∈ A denote the ground-truth optimal action (or the actual action chosen by a demonstra-

E.4. Theoretical Unification: The Oracle-Divergence Principle
Thus far, we have analyzed memory efficiency through specific metrics: Accuracy (PMI) and Certainty (Entropy). In
this section, we situate these metrics within a broader information-theoretic framework. We propose that the fundamental
objective of the memory module is to minimize the information-geometric distance between the agent’s policy and the ideal
policy.
We term this the Oracle-Divergence Principle.

E.4.1. T HE G ENERAL O BJECTIVE : D IVERGENCE R EDUCTION
Let Q denote the Oracle Distribution (or the “God View”), representing the ideal policy for the current state s. The goal of
memory generation is to transform the agent’s prior belief Pbase into a posterior Pmem that is statistically closer to Q.
We quantify this improvement using the reduction in Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence. The generalized Oracle
Information Gain, ∆div , is defined as:


                                      ∆div (m) = DKL (Q ∥ Pbase ) − DKL (Q ∥ Pmem ).

Since the Oracle distribution Q and the baseline prior Pbase are fixed for a given instance, maximizing this gain is equivalent
to minimizing the divergence of the posterior:

                                       arg max ∆div (m) ≡ arg min DKL (Q ∥ Pmem ).
                                            m                      m

Ideally, if the memory is perfect, Pmem converges to Q, and the divergence becomes zero.

E.4.3. T HE E NTROPIC C OROLLARY
This generalized perspective also explains the role of Entropy Reduction (∆H).
Since the Oracle distribution Q (One-hot) has an entropy of zero (H(Q) = 0), any policy Pmem that successfully minimizes

                                             Pmem → Q =⇒ H(Pmem ) → 0.

Therefore, Accuracy (PMI) and Certainty (Entropy) are not independent objectives. They are coupled features of the same
optimization process:

  • PMI measures the alignment of the probability mass with the peak of Q.

  • Entropy measures the compactness of the distribution, which is a prerequisite for resembling Q.

E.4.4. S UMMARY.
In this unified view, our framework offers a dual-layered approximation of the theoretical trajectory ∆div :

 1. The Accuracy Gain Efficiency Proxy (ρ): By assuming the standard One-hot Oracle, the Pointwise Mutual Information
    ρ serves as the primary computational metric. It provides an exact, low-cost measurement of the agent’s transport
    toward the optimal action a∗ , making it sufficient for large-scale performance benchmarking.

 2. The Validated Certainty Gain Proxy (ρΦ ): The Distributional Density ρΦ acts as a diagnostic complement. It
    approximates the entropy-minimization requirement of the Oracle-Divergence, explicitly safeguarding against “off-
    target” confidence (where the agent minimizes entropy toward a wrong distribution Q′ ̸= Q).

Thus, future memory research and prompting work can employ ρ to measure how much the additional tokens (e.g., the

Setup HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018) is a multi-hop question answering benchmark that is widely used to evaluate
multi-step retrieval and reasoning in RAG systems and agentic memory frameworks. Following the evaluation protocol of
HippoRAG2 (Gutiérrez et al., 2025), we use their preprocessed subset containing 1,000 examples. For all methods, we adopt

model, with decoding parameters max-tokens = 2048, temperature = 0.0, top-p = 1.0 During retrieval, we set top-k = 10,
returning the 10 most relevant memory nodes for downstream reasoning.

Episodic memory standardization. P LUG M EM standardizes agent trajectories into RL-inspired episodic tuples
⟨ot , at , st , rt , it ⟩ (observation, action, state, reward, intent/subgoal). For HotpotQA, the corpus is indexed passively
rather than generated by an acting agent. We therefore treat each unit of corpus text as a single-step “trajectory” (i.e., T = 1):
each episodic item contains one observation o1 (the text unit being indexed), while action-related fields are not instantiated
by execution. Concretely, we keep the unified tuple interface by setting a1 , s1 , r1 , and i1 to an empty (or N/A) placeholder.
The semantic extraction and retrieval primarily operate on the observation content.

HotpotQA multi-hop retrieval control. At each hop t, the retriever gathers a pool of candidates via the two-channel
update: i) link-based expansion via abstract nodes, where the retriever infers a small set of abstract concepts from current
query Qt , matches them to high-level concept nodes in the memory graph, and expands to adjacent low-level proposition
nodes via membership edges; ii) embedding-based retrieval from query Qt directly.
We then invoke an LLM controller to assess whether the currently retrieved candidates are sufficient to answer the question.
If the controller returns enough=true, we terminate early and pass the current fact set to the downstream QA model.
Otherwise, the controller selects a small subset of the most promising candidates to drive the next hop; in our experiments
we cap this subset at top-2 candidates. To form the next-hop query, we integrate the selected candidates with the previous
query. This integration can be performed by an LLM-based re-writer; however, we find that a simple concatenation of the
query and selected candidates performs comparably well in practice, and we therefore adopt concatenation as the default for
efficiency.

G. Analysis of the Structuring Module
The structuring module is the core component that realizes the memory-to-knowledge abstraction in our system. As it
operates at the very upstream stage of the pipeline, its effectiveness fundamentally determines the quality of all downstream
processes, including retrieval and reasoning. Given that our overall memory system consistently outperforms existing
baselines, it is important to further examine whether this module itself is reliable, accurate, and robust in practice.

                                                                 51

In this section, we focus on analyzing the behavior of the structuring module, with particular emphasis on the knowledge
extraction step. Our goal is to understand whether the module can accurately extract decision-relevant knowledge from raw
experience, and whether potential imperfections in this process affect downstream performance.
Our analysis leads to two key findings. First, we observe that modern LLMs already achieve strong performance in
knowledge extraction, resulting in a very low rate of critical factual errors that could affect final decisions. Second, to
account for potential imperfections, we explicitly design a set of fallback mechanisms that mitigate extraction errors. These
mechanisms provide substantial recovery capability, ensuring that even when extraction is incomplete or imperfect, the
system remains robust and maintains strong performance.

G.1. Design of the Structuring Module
We design the structuring module based on three core steps:
i) Standardization. Raw interaction trajectories are first converted into standardized episodic units (state, action, subgoal,
reward), enabling consistent processing across heterogeneous tasks.
ii) Knowledge Extraction. Semantic and procedural knowledge are extracted into structured forms:

  • Semantic knowledge is represented as propositions with associated concepts, forming a semantic graph.

  • Procedural knowledge is represented as intent–prescription pairs, forming a procedural graph.

iii) Graph Construction and Linking. The extracted knowledge is organized into a unified memory graph:

  • Relational edges connect concept–proposition and intent–prescription pairs.

  • Provenance edges link each knowledge unit back to its source trajectory, enabling grounding and verification.

To ensure robustness against potential upstream errors, we further introduce three layers of fallback mechanisms:

  • Comprehensive retrieval: Instead of relying on a single extracted memory, the system retrieves a ranked set of
    candidates.

  • Aggregation over experiences: Knowledge is induced from multiple retrieved candidates, reinforcing consistent
    signals while suppressing noise.

  • Episodic fallback: When inconsistencies arise, the system traces back to raw episodic memory (trajectory logs) to
    resolve conflicts.

These mechanisms ensure that potential imperfections in knowledge extraction do not propagate and degrade downstream
performance.

G.2. Evaluation Protocol for Structuring Quality
To systematically evaluate the quality of the structuring module, we introduce a categorization framework that classifies
extracted knowledge into three types: i) decision-sufficient, ii) helpful but imperfect, and iii) misleading.
This categorization provides a principled way to distinguish between successful and failed knowledge construction. Notably,
the “helpful but imperfect” category is defined under a strict criterion: even minor omissions of task-relevant details,
despite otherwise correct extraction, are classified as imperfect.
For LongMemEval and HotpotQA, we use three strong LLM evaluators (GPT-5.1, DeepSeek-V3, Sonnet-3.7) to assess all
instances, complemented by manual verification on 30 samples and all failure cases. For WebArena, we conduct full manual
evaluation due to the limited reliability of current VLM-based evaluation in complex web environments.

G.3. Empirical Analysis Across Benchmarks
(1) Structuring is near-perfect on knowledge-centric tasks.
On HotpotQA, we observe that misleading cases (hallucinations or factual errors) are below 2%, while imperfect cases
are below 5%, consistently across both LLM-based evaluation and manual verification. This is because the knowledge
units in this benchmark are fine-grained factual statements, which modern LLMs can extract reliably. Errors only occur in
rare cases involving long contexts and multi-step reasoning.
Importantly, we find zero cases where extraction errors lead to incorrect final answers. Instead, failures are primarily
due to the intrinsic difficulty of the benchmark, which is intentionally designed to make retrieval highly challenging. (In
fact, we observe that our system already approaches near-oracle performance given the retrieved context, indicating that
retrieval is already performing strongly.) This indicates that, in such settings, the structuring module is highly reliable and
not a bottleneck.
(2) Imperfect extractions are largely recoverable.
Under our strict evaluation protocol, LongMemEval exhibits less than 1% misleading cases and less than 14% imperfect
cases. We want to emphasize that this number largely reflects strict completeness requirements rather than critical errors. In
LongMemEval, there exists a significant mismatch between the format of the stored memory and the form of the query.
For example, a dialogue may contain a statement such as “I went to church on a specific day in December,” while the
corresponding question may ask “what faith-related activities did the user engage in during December.” This requires an
additional level of abstraction that is not explicitly present in the raw interaction. In standard knowledge extraction, such
transformations are not always directly inferred (e.g., mapping “going to church” to “faith-related activity”). Moreover, our
definition of incompleteness is intentionally strict: even if most relevant information is correctly extracted, the omission of a
single potentially useful element leads to classification as imperfect. This holds even when the missing information can be
recovered later through retrieval aggregation. As a result, the reported imperfect cases largely reflect this strict evaluation
criterion rather than substantial deficiencies in extraction quality.
We observe that approximately 65% of imperfect cases are recovered through retrieval aggregation fallback, meaning
that missing information is compensated by other retrieved knowledge. Furthermore, all factual errors are corrected by
fallback mechanisms. This demonstrates that the system is robust to incomplete extraction.
(3) Robustness under procedural tasks.
On WebArena, misleading cases are below 15%, mainly due to two well-known challenges in web agent research: i)
accurately understanding and summarizing complex web environments, and ii) reliably estimating whether a trajectory has
successfully completed a task (i.e., reward modeling). Both are active research problems in current foundation models.
Therefore, these errors primarily reflect limitations of the underlying models, rather than issues with the structuring module
itself.
Even in these cases, the system remains robust. Through retrieval aggregation, the agent can compare multiple experiences
and avoid being misled by individual errors. For example, in the GitLab subset, tasks with misleading memory still achieve
approximately 46% success rate, compared to around 39% without memory.
Further analysis shows that the primary bottleneck lies in the base agent’s interaction incapability with the environment
rather than the structuring module. In many failure cases, the structuring module functions correctly, but the agent struggles
to correctly interpret web pages and identify task-relevant information, or fails to execute actions reliably in the environment.
These issues are tied to the agent’s perception, decision-making, and action execution capabilities, as well as the underlying
foundation model’s understanding of the task, which leads to their failure to generate useful experience.
Overall, these results demonstrate that the structuring module is both accurate and robust in practice. It produces
high-quality knowledge representations with low error rates, and, importantly, its design ensures that potential imperfections
are effectively mitigated through fallback mechanisms. As a result, the module does not hinder system performance and
serves as a reliable foundation for downstream memory utilization.

H. LLM Routing Mechanism and P LUG M EM Usage Cost Analysis
In designing memory systems for LLM agents, it is important to recognize the inherent trade-off between computational
cost and performance. In general, increasing computational resources, such as using larger models, scaling up inference
time, or introducing multi-agent or search-based mechanisms, can lead to improved performance. However, such gains often
come at a disproportionately high cost, making them inefficient and less practical for real-world deployment.
In P LUG M EM, we explicitly aim to move beyond this conventional trade-off. Rather than improving performance by simply
increasing computation, our goal is to shift the Pareto frontier, achieving better performance at comparable or lower cost.
That is, we seek a design where the same level of computational budget yields stronger results, and where efficiency is
treated as a first-class objective rather than a secondary consideration.
To this end, we introduce a simple yet effective mechanism: LLM routing. Before processing a given memory-related
query, the system adaptively selects the most appropriate language model based on the estimated difficulty and requirements
of the task. For simpler operations, such as basic information extraction, smaller models are sufficient and more efficient.
In contrast, more challenging tasks, such as complex reasoning over long contexts or processing rich web observations,
are assigned to stronger models with higher capacity. This adaptive allocation allows the system to match computational
resources to task complexity, avoiding unnecessary overhead. In practice, this adaptive routing can be implemented with
lightweight heuristics. For example, short and structurally simple queries are typically handled by smaller models, while
longer inputs such as web environment or context-rich observations are routed to more capable models. Additionally, queries
can be grouped by type: by sampling a small subset of similar tasks and evaluating their difficulty with a stronger model, the
system can infer an appropriate routing strategy for the entire group. Such designs enable both simple and more advanced
versions of routing, while keeping the overhead of the adaptation process itself minimal.
Importantly, for fair comparison with prior work, all baselines are evaluated using a uniform strong-model setting, where
the same high-capacity model is used throughout the entire pipeline regardless of task difficulty. In contrast, P LUG M EM
with routing selectively invokes smaller models when appropriate. From a cost perspective, especially in self-hosted settings,
calls to smaller open-source models (e.g., 32B-scale models) incur negligible cost compared to large proprietary models,
making this design significantly more efficient.
We summarize the token usage cost and latency across methods in Tables 21, 23, and 24 (see the Additional Experimental
Details section F). From these results, we highlight three key observations: i) Under a uniform model setting, P LUG M EM
achieves superior performance while maintaining token consumption at a similar scale to competitive baselines, often not
being the most expensive despite delivering better results and richer memory representations; ii) With adaptive routing
enabled, P LUG M EM consistently uses the fewest closed-model tokens; iii) On simpler benchmarks, routing reduces closed-
model usage by two to three orders of magnitude. iv) During the memory graph retrieval stage, the system latency remains
comparable to other memory modules.
Overall, these results demonstrate that our design not only improves performance, but does so in a cost-efficient manner.
The integration of LLM routing within the memory module enables substantial reductions in computational cost while
preserving, and often enhancing, effectiveness.

Reflective Memory Management. RMM (Tan et al., 2025) introduces a novel reflective mechanism for long-term

I.2. HotpotQA
Inspiration from HippoRAG2. Our retrieval design is partly inspired by HippoRAG2, which demonstrates that intro-
ducing an explicit graph structure and a controlled multi-hop traversal can outperform purely similarity-based retrieval on
multi-hop QA. In particular, HippoRAG2 separates recognition (filtering/selecting relevant structured units) from expan-
sion (graph-based propagation to retrieve supporting evidence), reducing spurious hops and improving evidence quality.
P LUG M EM adopts this high-level principle but generalizes it to typed agent memory. We construct semantic and procedural
memory graphs and use abstract nodes (concepts/intents) as routing signals to activate specific proposition/prescription nodes,
enabling abstraction-to-specificity traversal. Moreover, analogous to HippoRAG2’s recognition step, PlugMem employs an
LLM-based controller to i) select memory types (G ET M ODE), ii) propose next-hop abstract signals (G ET P LAN), and iii)
decide early stopping or focus candidates under a fixed budget (M ULTI H OP C TRL). Finally, we integrate a two-channel

candidate update: link-based expansion through abstract nodes and embedding-based retrieval from a refined query, followed
by reranking/pruning and optional reasoning-based compression.

Test-time Scaling. Beyond the default configuration, P LUG M EM admits two practical test-time “knobs” that consistently
improve performance. First, we can increase the hop limit Tmax , effectively scaling the amount of retrieval computation
at inference time. This allows the retriever to chain additional abstraction–specificity steps and is analogous to test-time
scaling in reasoning-centric methods. We experiment with a subset on HotpotQA, when increasing the multi-hop limit Tmax
from 2 to 4, we observe a performance gain from 66.00/74.73 to 69.00/78.11 (measured in Exact Match/F1-Score).
Secondly, when forming the next-hop query Qt+1 , we optionally replace naive concatenation of (Qt , selected facts) with an
LLM-based query synthesizer that rewrites a focused and self-contained query. Empirically, synthesis-based refinement
reduces lexical drift and redundancy, and helps the embedding channel retrieve more targeted evidence in later hops. We
treat both mechanisms as controllable components within the retrieval controller, enabling a smooth accuracy–cost trade-off
depending on the task budget.

I.3. WebArena
Prior work designed specifically for WebArena often incorporates manually engineered domain-level instructions (or
“tips”) into the agent’s input context. A common pattern in task-specific adaptations, such as AWM (Wang et al., 2024b),
SteP (Sodhi et al., 2024), and more recently ColorBrowserAgent (Wang et al., 2026), is the inclusion of explicit guidance
that biases the agent toward particular interaction patterns within a site domain (e.g., navigation conventions or preferred UI
elements). In contrast, in our main experiments we deliberately avoid injecting such handcrafted instructions or explicitly
defined action policies. Instead, we rely solely on P LUG M EM ’s ability to automatically store, retrieve, and re-apply both
agent-generated and human demonstration trajectories as structured memory, allowing behavior to emerge from experience
rather than prompt engineering.

Further task adaptation We additionally conduct a targeted case study to examine whether P LUG M EM can benefit from
manual task adaptation when such domain-specific constraints are known. We identify a subset of WebArena tasks that
remain challenging for P LUG M EM and introduce a small set of manually designed instructions into the reasoning module.
Specifically, we observe that P LUG M EM occasionally instructs the agent to navigate via category dropdown menus when
the correct strategy in WebArena is to use the search bar. By incorporating the additional guidance shown in Prompt 18, we
resolve several previously failing cases (see Example 26) by explicitly conditioning the agent’s action selection. WebArena
uses strict, programmatic success criteria but concise queries, where otherwise reasonable strategies may be scored as
incorrect. Although P LUG M EM successfully retrieves relevant procedural knowledge for tasks such as “searching for teeth
grinding mouth guards,” it does not, by default, infer that certain plausible actions are disallowed by the benchmark’s
implicit rules. This case study demonstrates that P LUG M EM natively supports fine-grained manual conditioning when
necessary, while remaining effective without task-specific prompting in the general case.
